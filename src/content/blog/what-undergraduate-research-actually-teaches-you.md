Getting started with undergraduate research can feel overwhelming. You’re expected to read papers on topics you’re still trying to understand, implement methods you’ve only recently learned about, and figure out problems that don’t always have straightforward solutions.

Over time, you realize that research isn’t really about knowing everything. It’s about figuring things out, often through mistakes, questions, and a lot of trial and error.

## “Ask questions, and be honest about what you don’t know”

Your PI knows you’re an undergraduate. You’re not supposed to walk into a research lab knowing everything already. You’re there to contribute, learn, and improve along the way.

When I first started doing research, I realized that being technically sound wasn’t always enough. I was comfortable with the engineering side of things and could interpret technical concepts, but I sometimes struggled with understanding the bigger picture: what exactly needed to be done, how to approach a task, and what was expected of me. I learned that technical ability and knowing how to navigate a research project are two different things. Over time, I began to understand the importance of asking for clarity, communicating where I was stuck, and making sure I was working toward the right goal before diving into implementation.

If you don’t understand what you’ve been asked to do, ask. If a concept doesn’t make sense, say so. There’s no point pretending you understand something when you’re going to struggle with it later anyway.

Sometimes, a quick conversation with your PI can save you hours of working in the wrong direction. Be clear about where you’re stuck, explain what you’ve tried, and ask for guidance when you need it. You’re expected to make an effort on your own, but that doesn’t mean you have to figure everything out alone.

## “Get comfortable with trial and error”

You’ll probably have ideas that sound good in theory but don’t work when you actually try implementing them. That’s research.

An experiment might not give you the results you expected. Your implementation might keep breaking, or you might spend an entire afternoon trying to figure out why something that worked yesterday suddenly doesn’t work today.

It can be frustrating, especially when you feel like you’re making no progress. But don’t dismiss an approach before you’ve given it a fair shot. And when something fails, take the time to figure out why. Was your assumption wrong? Did you overlook something in the implementation? Was there a better way to approach the problem?

You won’t get everything right on the first attempt. Sometimes, the only way forward is to try something, see what happens, and adjust accordingly.

## “Read to understand, not just to finish papers”

It’s easy to convince yourself that you understand a topic because you’ve read five papers on it. But reading through a paper and actually understanding what the authors did are two very different things.

When you read, pay attention to the problem they’re trying to solve, why they chose a particular approach, and what assumptions their method relies on. Try to connect their work to what you’re doing rather than treating every paper as an isolated piece of information.

You’ll come across concepts that make no sense the first time around. Read those sections again. Look up unfamiliar terms, find another explanation, or work through the equations yourself if you have to.

There’s no need to rush through the literature just to increase your paper count. It’s much more useful to understand a few papers well enough to apply what you’ve learned than to read dozens and struggle to explain what they actually contribute.

## “Use AI, but don’t outsource your thinking”

AI can be genuinely helpful when you’re entering a research area you know very little about. It can explain unfamiliar terminology, walk you through difficult equations, help you understand an implementation, or give you a starting point when you don’t know where to look.

But there’s a difference between using AI to learn faster and using it to avoid learning altogether.

If you’re copying code you can’t explain or accepting explanations without checking whether they’re correct, you’re missing the point of the exercise. You might finish the task sooner, but you haven’t necessarily developed the skills you’ll need when something breaks or your PI asks you to justify a decision.

Use AI when you’re stuck, but make an effort to understand the answer you get. Check important claims against the original papers, test the code, and question explanations that don’t quite add up.

The goal is to become more capable of doing research, not simply to get research tasks done faster.

## “Give your work your full attention”

When you sit down to work on a problem, actually spend time working through it. Constantly switching between tasks makes it harder to follow your own reasoning, particularly when you’re dealing with unfamiliar concepts or debugging something complicated.

Start with the basics. Break the problem into smaller parts and make sure you understand each one before adding more complexity. When something goes wrong, change one thing at a time instead of making several changes and hoping one of them fixes the issue.

You don’t need to have everything figured out before you start. What matters is that you put genuine effort into the work, understand the decisions you make, and take responsibility for what you’re building.

## “Enjoy the process”

Research won’t always be rewarding in the moment. You might spend days trying to get something to work, only to discover that the approach wasn’t right in the first place. Sometimes, you’ll put considerable effort into an experiment and end up with results that don’t support your original idea.

That’s frustrating, but it’s also part of learning how to do research.

Try to appreciate the smaller moments, too. The first time an implementation finally works, when a difficult paper starts making sense, or when you figure out why an experiment failed. Those moments might seem insignificant compared to the final result, but they’re often where a lot of the learning happens.

You don’t need to start out as an expert, and you won’t become one overnight. Give yourself time to learn, make mistakes, and work through problems you don’t immediately know how to solve.

Stay curious. Ask questions when you need to. And remember that figuring things out is part of the job.
